## Introduction

A hydraulic oil press has few moving parts compared with a screw expeller, and under a sensible maintenance routine its frame, cylinder, and tabung press will outlast more than one ownership. The parts that do wear — seals, filter elements, valve internals — fail in predictable ways and give clear warning. Almost every expensive breakdown seen by service teams traces back to one of three neglected basics: contaminated hydraulic oil, an ignored leak, or an operator bypassing a safety interlock.

This guide is built around a calendar (daily, weekly, monthly, quarterly, annual) and then covers the hydraulic, electrical, and structural systems in detail, a troubleshooting table for the faults mills actually encounter, and a spare-parts stock list sized to prevent downtime rather than to fill a shelf.

## Maintenance Schedule at a Glance

| Frequency | Key Tasks | Time Needed |
|-----------|-----------|-------------|
| Daily | Clean machine and floor; check oil level and leaks; inspect tabung press residue; test guards/e-stop | 15–20 min |
| Weekly | Clean tank return filter; check fittings and hoses; tighten electrical terminals; lubricate pins/hinges | 30–45 min |
| Monthly | Check oil condition (sight/smell/sample); inspect seals; verify pressure gauge; calibrate temperature instruments | 1 hour |
| Quarterly | Inspect cylinder rod and frame welds; change oil if hours reached; full e-stop and interlock test | Half day |
| Annually | Full hydraulic service; valve block inspection; structural bolt torque; electrical insulation test | 1 day |

In a double-shift operation compress these intervals by calendar time and follow operating hours instead.

## Daily Maintenance

- Wipe down the frame, pressure plate, and tabung press area; oil on the floor is both a slip hazard and evidence of a leak worth finding.
- Check hydraulic oil level in the sight glass with the ram retracted; top up with the correct grade if low, and investigate if the same machine needs topping up again within a week.
- Inspect the pressing chamber and tabung press slots for compacted residue; baked-on fines reduce oil outlet area and lengthen cycles.
- Confirm the emergency stop stops the pump and that guards are in place before starting.
- Log pressure reached, cycle time, and anything unusual — a cycle that creeps from 35 to 45 minutes is usually the first warning of a tired filter or pump.

## Weekly Maintenance

- Remove and clean the hydraulic tank return filter / suction strainer.
- Walk every hydraulic connection with a clean piece of cardboard held below joints; tiny seeps show before they become leaks.
- Inspect hoses for chafing, hardening, or cracked covers, particularly where hoses move with the frame.
- Check and tighten panel terminals (power isolated and locked out), and inspect the power cable for damage.
- Lubricate barrel-slide rails, hinges, and any grease points per the machine plate.
- Clean the motor cooling fins and confirm the fan cowl is intact.

## Monthly Maintenance

- Assess hydraulic oil: it should be clear amber (ISO VG 46 anti-wear hydraulic oil). Milky oil means water contamination; dark, burnt-smelling oil means heat degradation; glitter on the drain plug means metal wear. Any of the three calls for a change and a search for the cause.
- Inspect cylinder and pump seals, valve gaskets, and O-rings; replace at the first sign of weeping rather than after failure.
- Verify the pressure gauge against a known gauge or test point; an over-reading gauge tempts operators to under-press, and an under-reading gauge invites overload.
- Check temperature switches and roaster thermocouples against a reference thermometer.
- Review the month's batch log for drift in cycle time or peak pressure.

## Quarterly and Annual Maintenance

- Replace hydraulic oil at **every 2,000 operating hours** (or at least annually for lightly used machines); drain hot, clean the tank bottom and magnets, refill through a filter.
- Inspect the exposed cylinder rod for scoring or pitting; a damaged rod destroys seals rapidly — protect it with the gaiter and never leave the ram extended when idle.
- Inspect frame welds, column nuts, and structural bolts on high-pressure models; re-torque to the factory values after the first month of a new machine and annually thereafter.
- Full functional test of e-stop, door interlocks, pressure relief valve, and (where fitted) over-pressure protection.
- Annually: inspect the valve block and pump listening for cavitation noise, and run a megger insulation test on the motor.

## Hydraulic System Maintenance

### Hydraulic Oil Management

Hydraulic oil is a machine component, not a consumable to buy on price alone.

- Use **ISO VG 46 anti-wear hydraulic oil** (or the equivalent specified on the machine plate).
- Ideal operating temperature: **30–50°C**. Below 20°C oil is thick and cycles are slow; above 60°C oil degrades quickly and seals harden. In hot climates, add cooling or shade; in cold climates, idle the pump before pressing.
- Change at 2,000 hours or annually, whichever comes first, and always after a pump failure or major seal failure to flush out debris.
- System capacity is roughly 30–40 litres on HN325/HN365-class machines and 50–60 litres on HN426/HN500-class frames — confirm against the plate before ordering oil.
- Store sealed drums indoors; dust and water in the drum are the commonest source of contamination introduced during a supposedly correct oil change.

### Cylinder Care

- Keep the rod clean and its protective gaiter fitted; wipe the rod before full retraction if the press is in a dusty room.
- Do not use the machine with a weeping rod seal — a small leak becomes a scored rod, and a scored rod means far more than a seal replacement.
- Rod surfaces showing light marks should be dressed per the factory's guidance; deep marks require replacement.

### Filters

- Clean the return/suction filter every 50–100 operating hours (weekly for single-shift mills).
- Replace elements that are damaged, distorted, or that rapidly re-clog after cleaning.
- Keep a genuine-spec element in stock; an over-porous substitute lets particles into the valve block where repairs are expensive.

## Mechanical and Structural Care

- Inspect tabung press slots for peening or deformation; compare oil flow with commissioning performance. Bar-type slotted tabung press are designed to clear themselves, but hardened residues between campaigns (especially coconut and cottonseed) should be removed with the recommended tools, never with a cutting torch.
- Check the pressure plate for scoring and the cake discharge surfaces for burrs that tear filter cloth or scratch nickel-plated interiors.
- On double-barrel machines, inspect the sliding rails and stops; misaligned stations are a frequent source of uneven cakes.
- Keep food-grade tabung press on coconut and cold-press lines protected from carbon-steel tooling; use plastic or stainless implements.

## Electrical System Maintenance

- Confirm supply voltage with the motor running (not just idle); chronic low voltage overheats windings.
- Keep motor cooling fins and fan clear; a hot motor is often simply a dusty motor.
- Clean the control panel with a dry cloth after isolation; check buttons, indicator lamps, and e-stop action.
- Re-torque terminals annually; thermal cycling loosens copper over time and loose terminals are a fire risk.
- See the [electrical requirements guide](/resources/oil-press-electrical-requirements-guide/) for earthing, breaker, and surge-protection detail.

## Safety Maintenance

- Test the e-stop and interlocks monthly and record the test; a safety device that has never been tested is assumed working, not known working.
- Lockout/tagout procedure for any internal work: isolate power → lock the switch → verify zero energy with a tester → release stored hydraulic pressure → tag the machine → perform the work → remove tools, refit guards, then restart.
- Relieve hydraulic pressure before opening any fitting; oil under residual pressure can inject through skin.
- Keep a fire extinguisher suitable for oil/electrical fires in the room; oil-soaked rags should be stored in a closed metal container.

## Troubleshooting Guide

| Symptom | Likely Causes | Actions |
|---------|---------------|---------|
| Low or slow pressure build-up | Low oil level; clogged suction filter; worn pump; internal relief valve drifting; cold oil | Top up; clean filter; warm system; check relief setting; pressure-test pump |
| Cycle longer than usual | Clogged filter; tired pump; worn seals allowing internal leak-down; low voltage | Work through hydraulic checks; measure voltage under load |
| External oil leak | Worn seal or O-ring; loose fitting; chafed hose; overfilled tank | Replace seal; torque fitting; re-route or replace hose |
| Pressure gauge stuck/erratic | Gauge failure; gauge-line blockage; pulsation damper fault | Replace gauge; check line; do not trust the reading meanwhile |
| Motor will not start | No supply; e-stop engaged; guard open; overload tripped; phase loss | Reset in order; check interlocks; investigate why the overload tripped before restarting repeatedly |
| Motor runs hot | Low voltage; dusty fins; single-phasing; overloaded cycles | Measure voltage; clean motor; check contactor and phases |
| Oil overheats | Continuous high-pressure hold; low oil level; hot room; cooler fault | Schedule breaks between long cold cycles; top up; ventilate/shade |
| Noisy pump (whine) | Cavitation from low level, blocked filter, or suction leak; worn pump | Check level and suction path; replace pump if bearings are scored |

## Spare Parts Inventory

A pragmatic stock policy for a single-press mill:

| Category | Items | Suggested Stock |
|----------|-------|-----------------|
| Critical (stops production) | Pump seal kit, main O-ring set, pressure gauge, return filter element | 1–2 each |
| Wear parts | Cylinder rod seal, wiper seal, valve O-rings | 2–3 sets |
| Hoses | The two highest-stress hoses made to length | 1 each |
| Consumables | ISO VG 46 oil, filter cloth/paper, cleaning supplies | 3–6 months |
| Electrical | Control fuses/indicators, e-stop, contactor coil where used | 1 each |

Remote locations should double the critical list and include a small torque wrench, filter spanner, and the machine's wiring and hydraulic diagrams. Parts supplied with the machine at commissioning cover the first running period; re-order when the stock is first used, not when it is empty.

## Records Worth Keeping

A single notebook or spreadsheet per machine, updated daily, beats sophisticated software unused. Track: running hours, oil top-ups and changes, pressure and cycle trends, replaced parts with dates, and operator names. This record both predicts failures and is the first thing a good technician asks for — it turns "it has been acting strangely for weeks" into a precise diagnosis.

## Conclusion

Maintenance on a hydraulic press is mostly about respecting three fluids and three checks: keep the hydraulic oil clean, cool, and at the correct level; check leaks daily, filters weekly, and the cylinder rod monthly; and never disable the safety devices that protect both the operator and the warranty.
