## Introduction

Seed is 80–90% of the cost of a litre of pressed oil. That single fact explains why yield matters more than the purchase price of the machine: one or two extra percentage points of extraction, repeated over every batch for a year, is worth far more than any discount on the press. The good news is that roughly half of the yield gap between an average mill and a well-run mill has nothing to do with buying a bigger machine — it comes from discipline in cleaning, moisture, heat, particle size, and pressure timing.

This guide explains where yield is won and lost, gives target numbers for each major seed, and ends with a troubleshooting table for the yield problems mills actually encounter.

## Understanding Oil Yield

Oil yield is the percentage of oil extracted from the raw material:

**Yield (%) = (weight of oil extracted ÷ weight of raw material) × 100**

Two related numbers are worth separating:

- **Total oil content** — what chemistry says is inside the seed (sesame 45–55%, peanut 45–50%, walnut 60–70%).
- **Extractable yield** — what mechanical pressing actually recovers in one pass. The difference stays in the cake: a good hydraulic line leaves 6–8% residual oil in cake; old drilled-barrel presses often leave 10–12%.

You can never press out all the oil a laboratory can measure. The practical goal is to move your yield as close to the seed-specific expected range as possible and to keep the residual oil in cake stable and low.

## Step 1: Start with Better Seed

No setting on the press rescues bad raw material.

- Buy fully matured, freshly harvested seed; immature kernels have formed less oil.
- Reject moldy, insect-damaged, or rancid lots — they lower yield *and* create food-safety problems (aflatoxin risk in peanut and cottonseed).
- Store seed at below 12% moisture, under 25°C, with ventilation; oil content does not recover after poor storage.
- Ask suppliers for oil-content or test-press results when tonnage is large; a 2% oil-content difference between suppliers dwarves the price difference per tonne.

## Step 2: Clean and Sort Thoroughly

Stones and hulls add dead weight to the tabung press, absorb oil, and prevent even pressure.

- Vibrating screen, magnetic separator, and destoner in sequence
- Target: under 0.5% impurities entering the press
- Gravity separation also removes light, empty kernels that carry little oil
- For peanut, hand or optical sorting of discoloured kernels protects both yield and food safety

## Step 3: Dehull Where It Pays

| Material | Recommended Hull Removal | Why |
|----------|--------------------------|-----|
| Peanut | 95%+ | Hulls absorb oil and scratch the tabung press |
| Sesame | 98%+ (for light oil) | Hull-on gives darker, stronger-flavoured oil |
| Sunflower | Strip most hulls | Waxy hulls cloud the oil |
| Walnut/almond | 100% shell removal | Shell fragments are hard and oil-free |

## Step 4: Condition Moisture to the Seed and Process

Moisture changes how oil moves through crushed tissue. Too wet and oil binds with water into a paste; too dry and the meal bridges in the tabung press without releasing.

| Material | Hot Press Moisture | Cold Press Moisture |
|----------|-------------------|---------------------|
| Sesame | 5–6% | 7–8% |
| Peanut | 5–7% | 7–9% |
| Rapeseed | 6–8% | 7–9% |
| Walnut/almond | — | 7–9% |
| Coconut (copra) | 5–7% | 6–8% |

Measure with a moisture meter every batch. If seed is too dry, add a fine water spray 30–60 minutes before pressing so moisture distributes evenly; if too wet, dry with hot air or clean sun drying.

## Step 5: Crush to the Right Particle Size

- Most seeds: 2–4 mm grit; hard nuts: 3–5 mm
- Grit that is too coarse leaves oil cells unopened and lengthens the cycle
- Grit that is too fine compacts into impermeable flour — oil literally cannot escape, and cycle times rise while yield falls
- If cakes come out dusty and tabung press slots blind frequently, crush coarser

## Step 6: Optimise the Hot-Pressing Process

### Roasting

Roasting is where hot pressing earns its extra yield: heat ruptures cells and lowers oil viscosity so it flows more freely.

| Seed | Temperature | Time | Moisture Out |
|------|-------------|------|--------------|
| Sesame | 160–180°C | 25–35 min | 3–5% |
| Peanut | ~130°C (120–150°C) | 20–30 min | 3–5% |
| Rapeseed | 150–170°C | 20–25 min | 4–6% |

Roast evenly, not maximally. Scorched seed produces dark, bitter oil that buyers reject — a total loss that no yield gain compensates.

### Pressure Programme

Hydraulic pressing works best as a curve, not a single shove:

1. Start at low pressure to seat the load and let trapped air escape.
2. Raise pressure in steps, holding each step; oil flows in waves rather than continuously.
3. Hold maximum pressure for 15–20 minutes until oil flow slows to drips.
4. Release slowly so the cake does not re-absorb oil.

Standard hot-press cycle: **30–40 minutes per batch**. Ending the cycle early to chase throughput leaves measurable oil in the cake.

## Step 7: Optimise Cold Pressing

Cold pressing trades some yield for quality, but the gap is smaller on a properly built high-pressure machine.

- Keep meal and tabung press below 60°C throughout; static hydraulic pressure adds no friction heat, which is the method's advantage over screw expellers.
- Cold-press needs higher cake-surface pressure to break unheated cells: 42 MPa or more — the territory of the [HN426](/products/cold-press-hydraulic-oil-press/) (42 MPa) and HN500 (52 MPa).
- Cycle runs about 2 hours per batch; do not shorten it, because unheated oil moves slowly.
- Keep moisture slightly higher (7–9%) than for hot pressing.
- Realistic cold-press yields: sesame 42–48%, peanut 38–42%, rapeseed 32–36%, walnut 45–50%.

## Step 8: Recover Oil in Filtration Without Losing It

- Primary filtration at 100–200 mesh, fine at 200–300 mesh
- The sludge collected on filter paper and in settling tanks is not waste: collect it, settle it, and recover the oil fraction (or repress accumulated fines in a cloth-lined small batch)
- Settle oil 24–48 hours before decanting; warm rooms (25–30°C) help fines drop faster

## Step 9: Measure, Record, and Compare

You cannot improve what you do not weigh.

- Weigh seed input, oil output, and cake for every batch.
- Record seed lot, moisture, roast temperature/time, cycle time, and peak pressure.
- Track yield per seed type on a simple weekly chart; drift downward usually signals dull crushers, worn tabung press slots, or operator shortcuts before it becomes obvious in the oil.
- Compare against the expected ranges in the [raw materials guide](/resources/oil-press-raw-materials-guide/), not against other mills with different seed and machines.

## Yield Benchmarks by Material

| Material | Oil Content | Hot Press Yield | Cold Press Yield |
|----------|-------------|-----------------|------------------|
| Sesame | 45–55% | 48–54% | 42–48% |
| Peanut | 45–50% | 45–50% | 38–42% |
| Rapeseed | 40–45% | 38–42% | 32–36% |
| Soybean | 18–22% | 18–22% | 15–18% |
| Walnut | 60–70% | Not recommended | 45–50% |
| Coconut (copra) | 60–70% | 60–65% | 55–60% |

(The hot-press yield of sesame can approach or exceed its quoted average oil content because ranges vary by variety and moisture basis; treat the figures as field ranges, not constants.)

## Common Problems and Solutions

| Symptom | Likely Cause | Corrective Action |
|---------|--------------|-------------------|
| Low yield across all batches | Moisture out of range; dull crusher; pressure released too early | Meter every load; service crusher; extend hold time |
| Low yield only with one seed lot | Poor or immature seed | Reject/replace lot; review supplier |
| Cake comes out oily and soft | Too much moisture; particle size too fine; insufficient pressure | Dry to target; crush coarser; check pump and seals |
| Oil is cloudy | Fines passing filter; water contamination | Replace filter media; dry seed; extend settling |
| Cycle time keeps growing | Tabung Press slots fouled; filter clogged; hydraulic oil tired | Clean tabung press slots; service filter; change ISO VG 46 oil |
| Yield drops in cold weather | High oil viscosity; cold hydraulic oil | Warm the work area; let the hydraulic system idle to 30–50°C |

## Conclusion

Maximising oil yield is a chain of small, measurable decisions: buy oil-rich seed, clean it properly, hit the moisture target for the process, roast evenly or keep genuinely cold, crush to a grit rather than a flour, follow a stepped pressure curve, and weigh every batch. Equipment sets the ceiling — a 42–52 MPa cold-press machine extracts what a lower-pressure one cannot — but day-to-day discipline decides how close you get to it. Run batch records for a month and most mills find 2–4 points of yield they were simply leaving on the table.
