## Introduction

The press machine gets most of the attention when a mill is planned, but every experienced oil maker will tell you the raw material decides at least half of the outcome — often before the seed reaches the barrel. Oil content sets the ceiling on yield; moisture and size decide how easily the oil flows; hardness decides the pressure needed; and the market price of the oil decides whether the whole exercise is profitable. Two mills running identical machines can earn very different margins simply because one buys mature, clean, well-stored seed and the other does not.

This guide profiles the seeds and nuts most commonly pressed on hydraulic lines, explains the properties that matter, and gives preparation targets for each material so that the machine and the ingredient are matched.

## The Five Properties That Matter

1. **Oil content** — the laboratory percentage of oil in the kernel; higher content means more saleable oil per kilogram and more forgiving economics.
2. **Moisture at pressing** — too dry locks oil in the meal; too wet creates sludge and emulsions. The target changes between hot and cold pressing.
3. **Kernel size and shape** — determines crushing gap and whether material bridges in the barrel.
4. **Hardness and shell content** — hard shells and low-oil husks demand higher cake pressure and are often removed first.
5. **Market value and demand** — walnut, almond, and cold-pressed sesame sell on quality at premium prices; soybean sells on volume and is unforgiving of high processing costs.

## Common Oil-Bearing Materials

### 1. Sesame Seeds

- Oil content: 45–55%
- Optimal moisture: 5–6% hot, 7–8% cold
- Dehulling: 98%+ for light oil; hull-on for dark, traditional flavour
- Roasting: 160–180°C for 25–30 minutes
- Expected yield: 48–54% hot, 42–48% cold
- Suitable models: HN325, HN365, HN426

Sesame is the classic hydraulic-press seed: high value, strong aroma, and a ready market in tahini, halawa, and seasoning oil across Africa, the Middle East, and Asia. Small, clean seeds roast very evenly, which makes batching forgiving for new operators.

### 2. Peanuts (Groundnuts)

- Oil content: 45–50%
- Optimal moisture: 5–7% hot, 7–9% cold
- Dehulling: 95%+ hull removal
- Conditioning/roasting: around 130°C (120–150°C) for 20–30 minutes
- Expected yield: 45–50% hot, 38–42% cold
- Suitable models: HN325, HN365, HN426

Peanut oil is a volume staple in Africa and Asia. Roasted peanut oil carries a distinctive flavour that commands local loyalty; the trade-off is strict aflatoxin discipline in purchasing, storage, and sorting — see the [aflatoxin control guide](/resources/aflatoxin-control-guide/).

### 3. Rapeseed and Canola

- Oil content: 40–45%
- Optimal moisture: 6–8% hot, 7–9% cold
- Dehulling: 85–90% where equipment is available
- Roasting: 150–170°C for 20–25 minutes
- Expected yield: 38–42% hot, 32–36% cold
- Suitable models: HN325, HN365, HN500

Rapeseed is the backbone cold-press crop of European and North American artisan oil makers. Cold-pressed "00"-style oil is sold fresh and local; because the seed contains glucosinolates, seed quality and freshness matter for flavour more than for most crops.

### 4. Sunflower Seed

- Oil content: 40–50% (oil-type varieties)
- Optimal moisture: 6–8%
- Dehulling: recommended for a light, low-wax oil
- Roasting: 140–160°C for 20–25 minutes when hot pressing
- Expected yield: 38–45% hot, 34–40% cold
- Suitable models: HN325, HN365, HN426

Sunflower is forgiving, widely grown, and price-stable — a good second seed for mills that want to spread seasonality. High-oleic varieties support premium bottled cold-pressed oil.

### 5. Walnuts

- Oil content: 60–70% (kernel)
- Optimal moisture: 7–9%, cold press
- Shells removed completely before pressing
- No roasting — cold press only to protect polyunsaturated fats
- Expected yield: 45–50% cold
- Suitable models: HN426, HN500

Soft, oil-rich walnut kernels shear into paste inside screw machines and are sensitive to heat, which is why walnut oil is almost always a hydraulic, high-pressure cold-press product. It sells in small bottles to gourmet and cosmetic buyers; plan for cold storage of the finished oil.

### 6. Almonds

- Oil content: 50–60% (kernel)
- Optimal moisture: 7–9%, cold press
- Full shell removal; no roasting
- Expected yield: 42–48% cold
- Suitable models: HN426, HN500

Almond oil follows the same logic as walnut: premium culinary, cosmetic, and massage-oil markets justify the expensive kernel. Small, frequent batches under nitrogen or dark storage protect the unsaturated oil.

### 7. Coconut (Copra and Fresh)

- Oil content: 60–70%
- Optimal moisture: 6–8%
- Remove shell and husk; grate or cut to 1–2 mm
- Expected yield: 60–65% hot from copra, 55–60% cold (VCO)
- Suitable models: HN426, HN500 with a 600 mm food-grade stainless barrel

Copra is hard and abrasive — pre-cutting equipment and a large barrel matter. Virgin coconut oil (VCO) from fresh, dried, or cold-processed meat is a high-value export product with specific hygiene requirements; food-grade contact surfaces are non-negotiable.

### 8. Soybeans

- Oil content: only 18–22%
- Optimal moisture: 7–9%
- Dehulling: 90%+
- Roasting: 140–160°C for 25–30 minutes
- Expected yield: 18–22% hot, 15–18% cold
- Suitable models: HN426, HN500 (high pressure helps)

Soybean is the classic example of a crop where economics differ by method: its low oil content means small-scale pressing relies heavily on the value of the high-protein cake. At industrial volumes most soybean oil is solvent-extracted; hydraulic pressing makes sense for local, identity-preserved, or certified non-GMO niches.

### 9. Tea (Camellia) Seed

- Oil content: 30–40%
- Optimal moisture: 5–7% hot
- Remove shells
- Roasting: 150–170°C for 25–30 minutes
- Expected yield: 28–35% hot
- Suitable models: HN325, HN365, HN426

Camellia oil is a premium Chinese and East Asian cooking oil ("oriental olive oil"); the cake also sells into traditional markets, which helps the modest per-kilogram yield.

## Material Preparation Best Practices

### Cleaning

Remove stones, metal, soil, husks, and broken kernels before anything else. Aim for under 0.5% impurities; this single step protects the barrel, improves colour, and prevents off-flavours.

### Moisture Conditioning

- Too dry: spray a measured amount of clean water, rest 30–60 minutes, re-measure
- Too wet: hot-air dry or sun dry on a clean surface; never press damp seed into storage
- Always measure — finger-and-thumb judgement is unreliable within the 1–2% window that matters

### Roasting (Hot Pressing Only)

| Seed | Temperature | Time |
|------|-------------|------|
| Sesame | 160–180°C | 25–30 min |
| Peanut | ~130°C (120–150°C) | 20–30 min |
| Rapeseed | 150–170°C | 20–25 min |
| Sunflower | 140–160°C | 20–25 min |
| Soybean | 140–160°C | 25–30 min |

### Crushing

2–4 mm for most seeds; 3–5 mm for hard nuts; 1–2 mm for fresh coconut meat. Check the first cake after any change: a clean, firm cake that releases from the barrel says the particle size and moisture are in balance.

## Storage and Handling

- Store seed at 15–25°C, below 65% relative humidity, and below 12% seed moisture
- Keep lots separated and labelled; process within six months for best oil quality — sooner for high-oil nuts
- Rodent-proof silos or sealed drums, raised off the floor, with ventilation
- First in, first out; inspect weekly in warm, humid seasons
- Never mix fresh deliveries into old seed — moisture and mould spread through a silo quickly

## Matching Material to Machine and Market

A useful way to plan a product range:

- **Volume anchor:** sesame or peanut (hot press) — throughput, familiar flavour, steady local demand
- **Premium margin:** cold-press walnut, almond, or rapeseed — smaller bottles, higher price, shorter shelf-life commitments
- **Regional specialty:** coconut/VCO, camellia, mustard, or moringa — differentiated products tied to where you operate

This mix uses one flexible hydraulic line, spreads seasonal risk, and avoids competing head-to-head with commodity refined oil on price.

## Conclusion

The machine extracts the oil, but the seed defines how much oil there is to extract, how it will taste, and what you can charge for it. Match each material to its moisture target, roasting curve, particle size, and pressure class — HN325/HN365 for conventional hot-press seeds, HN426/HN500 for hard nuts and cold-press work — and buy, clean, and store as if the oil price depended entirely on those steps, because it largely does.
