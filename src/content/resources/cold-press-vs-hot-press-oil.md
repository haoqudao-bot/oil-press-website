## Introduction

Cold or hot is the first process decision in oil milling, and it shapes everything downstream: which equipment you buy, how long each batch takes, what the oil tastes like, how long it keeps, who buys it, and what you can charge. It is also one of the most misunderstood choices — cold pressing is not simply "the same process without switching the roaster on," and hot pressing is not the "lower quality" option. They are different production systems aimed at different products and markets, often run on the very same hydraulic press.

This guide explains both processes in detail, compares the resulting oil on the dimensions buyers care about, quantifies the yield and throughput trade-offs, and shows how successful mills combine the two rather than choosing a permanent winner.

## What Is Cold Pressing?

Cold pressing extracts oil using mechanical pressure alone, keeping the oil below approximately 60°C (140°F) throughout the cycle. Seed is cleaned, lightly conditioned to 7–9% moisture, and gently crushed; no roasting heat is applied. Because unheated oil is more viscous and unheated cells resist rupture, cold pressing relies on sustained high cake-surface pressure and a long static hold.

### Cold Pressing Process

1. **Cleaning** — thorough removal of stones, dust, husks, and damaged kernels; quality defects cannot be masked by roasting flavour.
2. **Moisture conditioning** — adjust to 7–9%; moisture slightly higher than hot pressing helps oil move without heat.
3. **Gentle crushing** — open the cells without generating friction heat or producing flour.
4. **Pressing** — static pressure with cake-surface pressure of 42 MPa or more for approximately two hours per batch.
5. **Fine filtration** — multi-layer filtration at 200–300 mesh (polishing to 500 mesh for bottled oil) to produce a bright, sediment-free product without heat treatment.

### Equipment Requirements

- High pressure: cold pressing starts where hot pressing stops — 42 MPa cake pressure on the [HN426](/products/cold-press-hydraulic-oil-press/), rising to 52 MPa on the HN500 for the toughest nuts.
- Bar-type slotted tabung press that drain oil quickly without clogging under long, cold cycles.
- No roaster in the line, but filtration and settling capacity must be good — cold oil settles slowly.
- Cold handling does not mean cold storage is optional: unsaturated cold-pressed oils need cool, dark storage and short distribution times.

## What Is Hot Pressing?

Hot pressing roasts conditioned seed before pressing. Roasting ruptures oil cells, lowers oil viscosity, evaporates moisture to roughly 3–5%, and develops the roasted, nutty aroma associated with traditional cooking oils. Oil then flows quickly under moderate pressure.

### Hot Pressing Process

1. **Cleaning and dehulling** — remove impurities and hulls (peanut hull removal 95%+).
2. **Moisture conditioning** — adjust to 5–8% before roasting.
3. **Roasting** — seed-specific: sesame 160–180°C for 25–35 minutes; peanut around 130°C for 20–30 minutes; rapeseed 150–170°C for 20–25 minutes.
4. **Crushing** — break to 2–4 mm grit while still warm.
5. **Pressing** — 27–30 MPa cake pressure on an HN325/HN365 completes a batch in 30–40 minutes; stepped pressure with a 15–20 minute hold.
6. **Filtration** — primary and fine filtration; settling for clarity.

### Equipment Requirements

- A roaster (drum or pan) matched to at least twice the pressing rate.
- A press capable of 27 MPa+ cake pressure; higher-pressure models simply finish faster and drier.
- Hot pressing is the throughput configuration of the same machines: an HN325 on full 100 kg batches hot-presses roughly 900–1,000 kg per 8-hour shift single-barrel, versus about 300–400 kg cold.

## Oil Quality Compared

| Aspect | Cold Pressed | Hot Pressed |
|--------|--------------|-------------|
| Process temperature | < 60°C | Seed roasted 130–180°C before pressing |
| Aroma and flavour | Mild, fresh, seed-true | Rich, roasted, robust |
| Colour | Pale, golden | Darker, deeper |
| Heat-sensitive compounds (vitamin E, phenolics, some phytosterols) | Best retained | Partially reduced |
| Free fatty acids | Low if seed is fresh | Generally low; roasting also binds some moisture |
| Smoke point | Lower — use for dressings and low/medium heat | Higher — suited to frying and general cooking |
| Shelf life | Shorter; oxidises faster | Longer; lower moisture and antioxidant effects of roasting |

Two clarifications matter commercially. First, "cold pressed" is defined by process temperature, not by oil colour — a well-filtered cold-pressed sesame oil is still pale gold. Second, cold-pressed oil is not automatically healthier for every use: it is simply less altered, which makes it premium as a dressing or finishing oil, while properly made hot-pressed oil is the correct, traditional product for high-heat cooking and often has a longer shelf life.

## Yield and Throughput Compared

| Material | Cold Press Yield | Hot Press Yield |
|----------|------------------|-----------------|
| Sesame | 42–48% | 48–54% |
| Peanut | 38–42% | 45–50% |
| Rapeseed | 32–36% | 38–42% |
| Walnut | 45–50% | Not recommended — heat damages the premium profile |
| Coconut | 55–60% (VCO) | 60–65% (copra) |

Hot pressing extracts roughly 4–8 percentage points more oil from conventional seeds and does so three to four times faster per batch. The cake is also drier and stores better. Cold pressing accepts lower throughput and yield deliberately, recovering the difference through price per litre.

## Economics of the Two Products

The processes compete on different business models rather than on [cost per litre](/resources/oil-press-machine-price-guide/):

| Dimension | Cold Press Line | Hot Press Line |
|-----------|-----------------|----------------|
| Equipment needed | High-pressure press, fine filtration | Roaster + standard press |
| Output per shift | Lower (~300–700 kg depending on tabung press) | Higher (~900–1,400 kg) |
| Selling channel | Health shops, gourmet, online, export, cosmetic | Markets, restaurants, bulk cooking oil |
| Price positioning | Premium, small bottles | Volume, larger containers |
| Inventory risk | Higher — short shelf life, sell fresh | Lower — oil keeps longer |
| Quality tolerance for seed | Unforgiving — every defect shows | Roasting masks minor flavour defects (not safety defects) |

A profitable cold-press plan therefore needs disciplined batch sizes, dark/cool storage, dated labels, and distribution that turns stock quickly; a hot-press plan competes on throughput, cake revenue, and route sales.

## When to Choose Cold Pressing

1. The target customer pays a premium for natural, unrefined, traceable oil.
2. The materials are walnuts, almonds, apricot kernels, flaxseed, hemp, or high-oleic seeds, whose value lies in delicate flavours and nutrients.
3. You intend to support organic, artisanal, cosmetic, or export claims that require documented low-temperature processing.
4. Volumes per seed are modest and variety across seeds is the business model.
5. You can organize fast, cool distribution rather than warehouse stock.

## When to Choose Hot Pressing

1. You serve a local cooking-oil market that expects the traditional roasted aroma of sesame or peanut oil.
2. Maximising daily throughput and oil recovery per kilogram of seed is the priority.
3. The oil is sold for frying and general cooking, where smoke point and shelf life matter.
4. Seed, labour, and capital favour volume over premium positioning.

## Running Both on One Line

Hydraulic presses are process-flexible: the same machine cold-presses in the morning and hot-presses after roasting in the afternoon. Many successful mills deliberately operate both:

- **Product ladder:** one cold-pressed, bottled premium brand and one hot-pressed bulk cooking brand from one facility.
- **Seasonal switching:** cold pressing for gift and health seasons; hot pressing for festival cooking-oil demand.
- **Material-specific routing:** walnuts and almonds cold only; sesame and peanut mostly hot; rapeseed and sunflower in both, by grade.
- **Seed-quality routing:** the cleanest, freshest lots go to cold-press where every defect is visible; standard lots go to hot press — never mouldy lots to either.

## Common Mistakes

- Calling briefly heated screw-expeller oil "cold pressed" — buyers and regulators increasingly check process temperature claims.
- Cold-pressing low-quality seed hoping premium packaging hides it; it does the opposite.
- Hot-pressing walnuts or flaxseed for yield, destroying the exact properties that justify the kernel price.
- Bottling cold-pressed oil in clear plastic on sunny shelves — oxidation destroys the premium before the customer opens it.
- Comparing yields between processes and concluding cold is "inefficient" instead of pricing it for a different market.

## Conclusion

Cold and hot pressing are complementary tools, not rivals on a single quality scale. Hot pressing maximises yield, speed, shelf life, and roasted flavour for the cooking-oil market; cold pressing maximises nutrient retention, fresh flavour, and premium positioning for delicate seeds and nuts. Most growing mills end up running both on the same hydraulic line — HN325/HN365 configured around roasting for volume products, HN426/HN500 holding 42–52 MPa for cold-press and hard-nut work — and pricing each product for what the process genuinely delivers.
